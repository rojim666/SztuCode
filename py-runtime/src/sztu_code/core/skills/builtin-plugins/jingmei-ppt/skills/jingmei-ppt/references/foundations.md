# Presentation design foundations

## Begin with a visual thesis

Describe the deck in one line, such as “analytical, compressed, and calm” or “ceremonial, contemporary, and warm.” Add one productive tension: rigorous but human, traditional but current, vivid but ordered. Use that tension to resolve design choices.

## Build hierarchy before decoration

Create four readable levels: claim, section label, evidence, annotation. A slide should still communicate its hierarchy in grayscale and at thumbnail size. Use color and motifs to reinforce hierarchy rather than substitute for it.

## Treat density as bandwidth

Choose density by the audience's reading behavior:

- Low: keynote, storytelling, emotional persuasion.
- Medium: internal update, workshop, sales narrative.
- High: executive review, research report, decision memo.

High density means more structured evidence, not smaller text. Prefer tighter grouping, aligned columns, shorter copy, small multiples, and explicit conclusions. Preserve local breathing room around the primary claim.

## Establish vertical rhythm

Think in bands rather than isolated coordinates:

- Context band: section label or navigation.
- Claim band: title, usually one line.
- Separation band: a visible pause before evidence.
- Evidence field: the main table, chart, image, or composition.
- Implication band: conclusion, action, or source.
- Footer safety band.

Use the full useful height. A common failure is a title pressed against the first panel while a large empty strip remains at the bottom. Correct it by increasing the title-to-content gap and extending or shifting the evidence field downward. Keep the footer visually separate.

As a starting rhythm for a 16:9 slide, let the title finish around the upper 10–13% of the page, begin the main field around 15–18%, and allow useful content to continue to roughly 88–92%. Adapt for large titles, full-bleed visuals, and sparse ceremonial slides.

## Control spacing at three scales

- Macro: margins, title gap, content field, footer.
- Meso: gaps between panels, columns, and chart/table groups.
- Micro: row height, label-to-value distance, bullet spacing, and text-box insets.

Keep related items close and unrelated items visibly separate. If rows feel loose while text feels small, reduce row height first and reinvest the space in larger type or stronger annotations.

## Use a role-based visual system

Define roles rather than a long list of tokens:

- Background and surface
- Primary text and secondary text
- Accent and emphasis
- Positive, caution, and risk
- Hairline and grid

Choose one dominant font behavior and one contrast behavior. Contrast may come from weight, size, width, serif/sans pairing, or a restrained monospaced accent. Avoid using every available weight.

## Design a background identity layer

Treat background pattern, texture, and ornament as a low-frequency identity layer rather than filler. Derive the motif from the subject: geography, material culture, architecture, craft, notation, or an abstract structural idea. A pattern should contribute identity, hierarchy, navigation, or spatial balance; remove it if it only occupies emptiness.

Assign the background one or more page roles:

- Cover anchor: a recognizable field, crop, or motif that carries the deck's identity.
- Section transition: a stronger or darker version that changes pacing.
- Edge ornament: a corner, border, or footer motif that leaves the reading field quiet.
- Field texture: faint paper, grain, contour, or repeat that prevents a sterile surface.
- Diagram substrate: a map grid, route trace, axis, or architectural geometry that helps organize content.

Control motif scale, placement, and contrast independently. Avoid visible pattern behind dense copy, tables, or chart labels. Prefer cropping and partial repetition to tiling one motif uniformly. Reuse a motif family across the deck, but vary scale and crop so pages feel related rather than cloned.

Use background strength as a narrative signal: strongest on the cover, section dividers, and deliberate contrast slides; quietest on evidence-heavy pages. Let faint motifs resolve corners, side margins, and the lower page without pulling the visual center of gravity away from the claim.

Choose implementation according to editability. Use native lines and simple shapes for structural motifs that should remain editable; use generated or sourced raster artwork for paper, ink, atmospheric, or culturally specific texture. Keep titles, evidence, captions, and sources as editable layers above the background.

## Compose evidence

Use charts for patterns, tables for exact comparison, text for interpretation, and imagery for context or emotion. Put the interpretation next to the evidence. Prefer a few strong alignments to many boxes. Use panels only when they clarify grouping; a grid of identical cards can make a static slide feel like software UI.

## Diagnose visual problems

Ask:

- Does the title state a claim or merely name a topic?
- Is the title clearly separated from the evidence?
- Is the visual center of gravity too high, low, left, or right?
- Does the bottom feel intentionally resolved?
- Are rows too loose relative to their type size?
- Can the smallest meaningful text be read in the intended venue?
- Are colors carrying semantic roles consistently?
- Does every decoration support identity, hierarchy, or navigation?
- Are adjacent slides too similar in silhouette?

## Preserve editability without looking generic

Use native text, tables, charts, and simple shapes when the deck will be reused. Create character through proportion, typography, line treatment, cropping, and rhythm rather than relying only on flattened artwork. When imagery is essential, preserve an editable text and data layer around it.

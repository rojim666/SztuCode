# Historical education recipe

## Visual thesis

Scholarly, narrative, and period-aware; historically evocative but classroom-clear. The deck should feel like a contemporary interpretation of source material, not a costume-drama poster or a simulated antique artifact.

## Communication job

Help learners connect chronology, institutions, geography, causes, and consequences. Prefer a learning progression: question -> context -> evidence -> causal explanation -> synthesis -> discussion.

## Palette and type

- Build on warm paper, ink black, mineral blue-green, restrained cinnabar, and muted gold.
- Let one mineral color dominate and reserve cinnabar for dates, turning points, routes, and contested claims.
- Create period character through a serif-led hierarchy rather than decorative pseudo-calligraphy. Use a Chinese Song-style serif for deck titles, slide claims, major dates, and synthesis statements; use a neutral Chinese sans serif for longer explanations, labels, and chart text.
- Use Kai-style or handwritten faces only for a very small number of short quotations, artifact captions, or marginal annotations. Never use them for long body copy or dense tables.
- As a flexible classroom starting point, use roughly 44–60 pt for the cover title, 30–36 pt for slide claims, 18–24 pt for subheads, 14–18 pt for projected body text, and 10–12 pt for sources or marginal notes. Adjust to venue and content instead of applying a global scale reduction.
- Favor deliberate contrast: compact serif headlines with generous line spacing, then denser sans-serif evidence below. Do not make every text role look “ancient.”
- Choose fonts that exist in the delivery environment. On macOS, Songti SC with PingFang SC is a practical pairing; for cross-platform delivery, prefer an installed Source Han Serif/Noto Serif CJK with its sans companion. Dual-render in PowerPoint and LibreOffice only when cross-platform delivery is requested or font substitution is a demonstrated risk.

## Background motif system

Derive motifs from the actual topic rather than a generic national style. Useful sources include maps and waterways, city walls and street grids, roof or lattice geometry, seal impressions, scroll edges, paper fiber, contour lines, measurement marks, and simplified motifs from verified objects.

- Cover: use one immersive background or large crop with a quiet title field. The motif may combine geography, architecture, and material texture.
- Section divider: intensify color or invert the palette; enlarge the same motif family.
- Content page: confine pattern to a corner, edge, footer, or a pale structural field. Keep the central reading area clean.
- Map or timeline page: let routes, contours, or grid logic become the composition substrate, not a decorative overlay.
- Closing page: echo the cover motif at lower contrast to create return and resolution.

Avoid generic dragons, excessive palace silhouettes, random cloud ornaments, fake aged manuscripts, and decorative borders on every side. Do not imply that invented artwork is an authentic historical source.

## Density and rhythm

Use medium density for classroom projection. Titles state the historical point; body text stays concise enough to explain aloud. Build a clear title-to-content gap and let the main evidence field reach into the lower page. Keep source notes visually separate.

## Slide vocabulary

- Chronology ribbon with turning points and short explanations.
- Simplified map or route composition with editable labels.
- Institution comparison using aligned columns or a hierarchy diagram.
- Cause-and-effect chain with evidence and consequences.
- Artifact or image-led close reading with two or three annotations.
- Classroom synthesis slide with a debatable question and evidence prompts.

## Image behavior

Prefer maps, architecture, landscape, objects, and historically grounded illustration. When generating atmospheric backgrounds, specify that the image is historically inspired and contains no text, watermark, fabricated inscription, or claimed authentic artifact. Keep explanatory labels editable in PowerPoint.

## QA questions

- Does the pattern originate from the topic rather than a generic cultural cliché?
- Is the central reading field quiet enough for projection?
- Can students distinguish chronology, cause, evidence, and interpretation?
- Does the lower page feel resolved without decorative clutter?
- Are historical claims and visual sources clearly traceable?
